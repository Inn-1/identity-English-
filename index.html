On Identity

Five texts, three expanded past summary into close reading and evidence, and one recorded conversation about who we are, and who we're allowed to be.

Begin reading
“No, insisted the kid, it was you, me, and everybody else. We made them that way.” Ian Adams, “The Lonely Death of Chanie Wenjack”

This anthology gathers five texts, a short story, a poem, a personal essay, a podcast episode, and an article, that each treat identity differently. Some show it being quietly erased. Others show it being protected at a real cost, or built out of whatever is left over after history gets in the way.

None of them treat identity as something stable that just sits there, waiting to be found. Three of these five texts get a full, expanded reading further down the page, and those same three are also discussed aloud in a recorded conversation, in my own voice, further down. Every text below includes a short excerpt and a direct link to the original source.

03Deeper Readings

Three of the five texts, expanded past summary and into close reading, evidence, and argument.

Short Story

The Story of an Hour

Kate Chopin · americanliterature.com ↗

When Chopin first published this story in Vogue in December 1894, she titled it “The Dream of an Hour.” She changed it to “The Story of an Hour” before it was republished the following year, and that small edit says something about what she wanted the ending to mean. A dream can be dismissed as unreal, something that dissolves the moment a person wakes up. A story is a sequence of events that actually happened. By choosing “story,” Chopin insists that whatever Louise Mallard experiences in that room, however brief, was not a delusion. It was real.

The scene where Louise recognizes her own freedom is staged with unusual care. She sits in an armchair facing an open window, and everything coming through it belongs to the language of rebirth: treetops “aquiver with the new spring life,” the “delicious breath of rain,” patches of blue sky breaking through storm clouds. None of this imagery has anything to do with her husband's death directly. Chopin is separating Louise's private revelation from any single external cause. The window becomes a threshold between two identities: the domestic one she has occupied for years, and the one she is only now allowed to glimpse.

A kind intention or a cruel intention made the act seem no less a crime.

Chopin is careful to show Louise resisting what she feels before she accepts it. “She did not stop to ask if it were or were not a monstrous joy that held her,” Chopin writes, and that word, monstrous, is doing real work. It tells us Louise has already absorbed the idea that a wife feeling free at her husband's death would be a moral failure. It would be easy to read this story as being specifically about the unfairness of nineteenth century marriage law, and it is that. But Chopin pushes further, toward a general theory of how identity gets erased: she writes that any imposed “private will,” kind or cruel, is “no less a crime.”

The final scene confirms how invisible Louise's inner life was to everyone around her. She comes down the stairs looking, in Chopin's words, “like a goddess of Victory,” carrying a version of herself nobody in the house has ever seen. Then Brently walks in, alive, and Louise dies instantly. The doctors' verdict, “the joy that kills,” is the cruelest misreading in a story full of misreadings. Nobody considers that what actually stopped her heart was the sudden, total loss of the one hour in her adult life when she got to exist as someone other than his wife.

Poem

We Wear the Mask

Paul Laurence Dunbar · poetryfoundation.org ↗

We wear the mask that grins and lies,
It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,
This debt we pay to human guile;
With torn and bleeding hearts we smile,
And mouth with myriad subtleties.

Why should the world be over-wise,
In counting all our tears and sighs?
Nay, let them only see us, while
We wear the mask.

We smile, but, O great Christ, our cries
To thee from tortured souls arise.
We sing, but oh the clay is vile
Beneath our feet, and long the mile;
But let the world dream otherwise,
We wear the mask!

The word that changes how this poem reads is in the first stanza: “debt.” Dunbar writes, “this debt we pay to human guile.” A debt isn't something a person chooses, it's something owed. That distinction matters, because it means the mask isn't simply clever protection the speaker invented for himself. It's a cost, imposed by a world that would use his real feelings against him if he let them show.

The rondeau form is doing more than just repeating the opening line. Traditional rondeaux follow a tight rhyme scheme, and Dunbar keeps his rhymes locked to essentially two sounds across all three stanzas, guile and eyes recurring in different words: lies, eyes, smile, subtleties, wise, sighs, while, vile, mile, otherwise. That narrow rhyme scheme means the poem has almost nowhere new to go sonically. It is boxed in by its own sound the same way the speaker is boxed into performing the same mask every day. Read out loud, the poem reveals how few resources the speaker seems to have, the same handful of rhymes, the same refrain, over and over, no room to modulate into a different emotional register even when the middle of the poem reaches toward something as urgent as crying out to Christ. The form isn't decoration. It is the poem enacting the exact kind of constraint its subject describes.

Nay, let them only see us, while we wear the mask.

There is a layer of irony here worth sitting with. Dunbar became famous largely for his dialect poems, verse written in an exaggerated approximation of Black Southern speech that white audiences and publishers in the 1890s found charming and marketable. Poems like “We Wear the Mask,” written in standard English, were the ones Dunbar considered more serious, but they earned him less attention and less money during his lifetime. In other words, Dunbar's own literary career required him to wear a version of the mask this poem describes, performing an identity a white publishing industry wanted to see, in order to be allowed a platform at all.

It's also worth noting that Dunbar wrote this poem in 1895, eight years before W. E. B. Du Bois published The Souls of Black Folk and gave English the phrase “double consciousness,” the sense of always seeing yourself through the eyes of a world that views you with contempt. Dunbar doesn't use that language, but the poem is clearly reaching toward the same idea. The mask is what happens when you know you're being watched by people who have already decided what they expect to see, and you have to manage that expectation just to move safely through your own day.

There is historical weight here too. Dunbar was writing in 1895, right as Reconstruction was collapsing into Jim Crow. For a Black man in that period, controlling what emotional information you gave to a hostile public wasn't weakness, it was survival. So the mask isn't only a symbol of hidden pain. It functions more like a strategy: the identity underneath hasn't disappeared, it's being deliberately withheld from people who haven't earned the right to see it.

Personal Essay

Rice for Thanksgiving

Jocelyn Fong · thisibelieve.org ↗

Fong opens with a detail that's easy to read past too quickly: her grandmother died of cancer four years before she wrote this, and that death is what actually ends the yearly gathering where this tradition used to happen. She is dealing with two losses stacked on top of each other: the historical loss of Chinese language and tradition in her family going back generations, and the much more recent, personal loss of the one relative who still held that shrunken inheritance together.

Her detail about checking the “Asian” box on government forms, even though she is “equally white,” is not a small aside. Until the 2000 United States Census, there was no option to select more than one race at all. Fong grew up inside that bureaucratic logic, where mixed identity wasn't officially recognized as something a person was allowed to claim.

I believe in rice and gravy because I am rice and gravy.

The essay's smartest move is the aside about the phrase “long time no see,” which Fong mentions came from a literal translation of a Chinese expression into English. That phrase isn't correct English, and it isn't Chinese anymore either. It exists because two languages collided and neither one fully absorbed the other. Fong is telling us, without saying it outright, that rice and gravy works the same way: not a compromise between two cultures, but a third thing, born from contact between them.

By the end, Fong stops talking only about her own family and makes a claim about the country itself, calling her mixed identity “a mark of progress... a collage of people with roots all over the planet, who, though not without strife, form something new and strong together.” That pattern, culture interrupted by policy and then reassembled into something unrecognized by either original source, is close to the actual mechanism by which American identity gets made in general.

05Annotated Bibliography

  1. Adams, Ian. “The Lonely Death of Chanie Wenjack.” Maclean's, 17 Oct. 2016, macleans.ca/society/the-lonely-death-of-chanie-wenjack/. Accessed 22 July 2026.

    Identity, this article argues, isn't inherent to a person, it's created through culture, family, and community. Chanie was sent to a residential school where that identity wasn't respected, and lost it the moment he lost his connection to his family and was forced to adapt to a different set of rules.

  2. Chopin, Kate. “The Story of an Hour.” American Literature, americanliterature.com/author/kate-chopin/short-story/the-story-of-an-hour. Accessed 22 July 2026.

    Chopin's ending stays deliberately ambiguous. Louise mourns the way a wife is expected to, then finds a private relief in her own solitude that she can only name as “free.” Her death from “the joy that kills” is really the loss of the one hour she wasn't tied to someone else's expectations.

  3. Dunbar, Paul Laurence. “We Wear the Mask.” Poetry Foundation, www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44203/we-wear-the-mask. Accessed 22 July 2026.

    The mask in this rondeau isn't pretense, it's survival. Smiling despite tragedy was the only way for African Americans to get through a world that would otherwise see them as inferior, so the identity underneath the mask isn't lost, it's protected by the performance.

  4. Fong, Jocelyn. “Rice for Thanksgiving.” This I Believe, thisibelieve.org/essay/24145/. Accessed 22 July 2026.

    Fong's family eats rice at Thanksgiving because of a history of exclusion: illegal immigration, no path to citizenship until 1943, no legal interracial marriage until her father was a teenager. She once mourned the “loss” of her Chinese heritage, but what actually happened was that her family's culture was given up under the weight of those rules, not lost on its own.

  5. Gladwell, Malcolm. “Carlos Doesn't Remember.” Revisionist History, season 1, episode 4, Pushkin Industries, 7 July 2016, www.pushkin.fm/podcasts/revisionist-history/carlos-doesnt-remember. Accessed 22 July 2026.

    Gladwell follows Carlos, an academically talented but economically disadvantaged student, to argue that identity and success depend on the opportunities available to a person, not just on ability. The episode also shows how specific events Carlos can barely remember still helped shape who he became.

06Reflection

My first version of this site used a lot of color, a cursor effect, and cards that flipped over. Once I sat with it, none of that was actually helping a reader understand the texts, it was just noise layered on top of them. The version I kept is quieter on purpose: one accent color, a serif font for headings and a plain sans font for everything else, and photos that stay in grayscale until a reader hovers over them. Most of the texts in this anthology show identity being lost or protected quietly rather than dramatically, so a loud, busy site would have worked against that idea instead of for it.

The smaller conventions mattered more than I expected. Formatting the MLA citations correctly, keeping punctuation consistent around quotations, and making sure no typo slipped into a pull quote weren't just details to avoid losing marks. If I'm going to argue that a single word like “debt” changes how an entire Dunbar poem should be read, the rest of the page needs to look like it was actually proofread, or that specific claim starts to look like a guess instead of something I can back up.

My voice comes through in two different ways depending on the piece. In this reflection, it's direct, I'm not analyzing someone else's writing, just saying what I actually think. In the podcast, it comes through in how far I push the analysis itself: working through the rhyme scheme in Dunbar's poem, the title change in Chopin's story, the history behind Fong's essay, out loud, in my own words, instead of stopping at summary. What I've taken from this project is that identity isn't something people simply have. Louise Mallard loses hers quietly inside a marriage nobody would call cruel. Dunbar's speaker protects his by paying for it every day. Fong has to build hers out of a Thanksgiving side dish because the original inheritance was interrupted before she was born. None of them get to discover identity once and move on, and I don't think that's true for me either.
